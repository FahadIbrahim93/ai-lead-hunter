# 🩹 Psychological First Aid (PFA) — A Practical Guide for Bystanders

## ⚠️ First: Your Own Safety Comes Before Anything Else

Before you approach anyone:

1. **Stop, Look, Listen.** Pause 5–10 seconds. Scan the environment. Do you see danger? Active threat? Fire? Structural collapse? Traffic? 
2. **If the scene is not safe, do not enter.** You cannot help anyone if you become a casualty yourself.
3. **Move only if there is immediate, obvious danger.** Otherwise, wait for professionals.

> *"Your first priority is to conduct a scene survey to ensure the area is safe for you and others."*
> — Managing an Emergency Scene: The Complete Guide to First Aid Response

---

## What Is Psychological First Aid?

PFA is an **evidence-informed approach** for helping people in the immediate aftermath of disaster, violence, or trauma. It is **not** counseling, therapy, or a psychological intervention. It is a humane, supportive response to someone who is distressed.

**Goal:** Reduce initial distress, foster short- and long-term adaptive functioning.

**Who can do it:** Anyone — there is no license required. It was designed for first responders, volunteers, and bystanders.

**Sources:** 
- [NCTSN PFA Field Operations Guide, 2nd Edition](https://www.nctsn.org/resources/psychological-first-aid-pfa-field-operations-guide-2nd-edition)
- [PTSD: National Center for PTSD — PFA Field Operations Guide](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/disaster_events/for_providers/psychological_first_aid.asp)

## The Core Principles (PFA is Built on These)

1. **Safety** — physical and psychological
2. **Calm** — foster it, don't escalate
3. **Connection** — help people connect with supports
4. **Self-efficacy** — empower people to help themselves
5. **Hope** — realistic optimism, not false promises

---

## What to Do: The Do's

### 1. Politely observe first; don't intrude.
Approach calmly. Say your name if appropriate. Ask simple, respectful questions:
- *"Are you okay?"*
- *"Can I help you find someone?"*
- *"Do you need water or a place to sit?"*

> *"Politely observe first; don't intrude. Then ask simple respectful questions to determine how you may help."*
> — NCTSN PFA Tips for Adults

### 2. Listen actively — don't force them to talk.
Let them lead. If they want to talk, listen. If they don't, that's fine. Silence is acceptable. You are not there to "fix" their feelings.

### 3. Meet practical needs within your capacity.
Water. A blanket. A place to sit. Helping them contact a loved one. Moving them away from the chaotic area to a quieter spot if safe. These small things matter.

### 4. Give simple, accurate information.
- *"Help is on the way."*
- *"The police are looking into it."*
- *"Here's where you can find your family."*
- Avoid speculation. Don't share rumors.

### 5. Connect them with social supports.
Family, friends, community, faith leaders, trusted organizations. Encourage them to reach out. Offer to help them make a call if they want.

### 6. Be culturally sensitive.
Not everyone expresses distress the same way. Some people are stoic. Some are expressive. Respect their ways of coping.

### 7. Help them feel calm — your own calm matters.
Breathe slowly. Move deliberately. Speak in a low, steady voice. Panic is contagious. So is calm.

---

## What NOT to Do: The Don'ts

### ❌ Don't force help on people.
If someone says no, respect it. You can say, *"I'll be nearby if you need anything."*

### ❌ Don't force them to tell their story.
You do not need to hear what happened to them. Asking them to recount the trauma can re-traumatize. Let them decide if and when to share.

### ❌ Don't make promises you can't keep.
*"Everything will be fine."* You don't know that.  
*"You're going to be okay."* You don't know that.

Say: *"I'm here with you right now."* That's true, and it's what matters.

### ❌ Don't pathologize normal reactions.
Shock, numbness, crying, anger, confusion, dissociation — these can all be normal responses to abnormal events. You are not a clinician. Don't diagnose. Don't label.

### ❌ Don't give medications, food, or drinks to someone who is unconscious or disoriented.
Only do what you are trained and equipped to do. If you have first aid training, use it. If not, focus on PFA — emotional support, practical assistance, connection.

### ❌ Don't take photos or record people in distress.
This is exploitative and violates dignity. Put the phone away.

### ❌ Don't assume everyone who is quiet is fine — and don't assume everyone who is upset needs intervention.
Some people go quiet as a coping mechanism. Some people are crying and will be fine in an hour. Follow their lead. Offer. Don't push.

## Common Reactions After Trauma (and How to Respond)

People react differently. Here are common reactions drawn from the NCTSN PFA Tips for Adults handout, and how to respond.

| Reaction | What it might look like | How to respond |
|---|---|---|
| **Shock / numbness / denial** | Quiet, disconnected, "I can't feel anything," staring | *"You're safe now. Take your time. I'm here."* — Don't push them to "come out of it." |
| **Fear / anxiety** | Trembling, scanning the environment, can't sit still | Offer water, a quieter spot if safe, a chance to call someone. Speak slowly and calmly. |
| **Sadness / grief / crying** | Crying, withdrawing | It's okay to cry. Don't tell them to stop. Sit with them. *"It's okay. Take your time."* |
| **Anger** | Yelling, irritability, lashing out | Stay calm. Give space. Don't argue back. *"I can see this is really hard. I'm here if you want to talk or if you need something."* |
| **Guilt** | *"I should have done something,"* *"Why did I survive?"* | Normalize without minimizing. *"A lot of people feel that way after something like this. You don't have to figure it out right now."* |
| **Confusion / disorientation** | Repeated questions, not knowing where they are, not knowing what day it is | Give simple, repeated information. *"You're in [place]. Today is [day]. Help is on the way."* |
| **Physical symptoms** | Nausea, shaking, chest tightness, headache | Offer water, a place to sit, slow breathing. These can be normal stress responses. If severe or worsening, escalate to medical help. |
| **Wanting to find loved ones urgently** | Repeatedly asking about family, children, friends | Don't make promises about who is safe. Help them figure out how to find out — official information channels, reunification sites, family notification services if available. |
| **Wanting to be alone** | Pushing people away, isolating | Respect it. Say *"I'll give you space. I'm nearby if you need anything."* Check in periodically without crowding them. |

> Source: [NCTSN PFA: Tips for Adults](https://www.nctsn.org/resources/pfa-tips-adults) — a handout from the PFA Field Operations Guide providing common reactions after a disaster and ways to respond.

## The PFA "Look, Listen, Link" Framework

This is the practical core of PFA:

### Look
- Check for safety.
- Check for people with obvious urgent needs (medical, emotional).
- Observe who is most distressed — but remember, appearances can be misleading.

### Listen
- Ask needs and concerns.
- Listen to the person's story (only if they offer it).
- Listen for what they need right now.

### Link
- Link them to basic needs (water, shelter, information).
- Link them to loved ones and social supports.
- Link them to services — but only if available and appropriate.

## Self-Care for the Helper (What the PFA Guides Emphasize)

Helping in the aftermath of trauma affects you too.

- **Acknowledge your own feelings.** You may feel scared, sad, angry, helpless. That's normal.
- **Take breaks.** You cannot help if you are depleted.
- **Connect with your own support system.** Talk to someone you trust after.
- **Don't work alone if you can avoid it.** Buddy up.
- **Know your limits.** If someone is in acute psychiatric crisis (suicidal, psychotic, completely dissociated), professional help is needed. Connect them to it — you are not the right person for that.

---

## When to Escalate / Get Professional Help

Call emergency services or flag to a first responder if:

- Someone is a danger to themselves or others.
- Someone is unconscious or unresponsive (this is a medical emergency, not PFA).
- Someone is experiencing severe dissociation, psychosis, or acute panic that isn't settling.
- Someone has visible serious injury that needs medical care.
- You are unsure — it's always okay to ask a professional to assess.

For mental health crisis hotlines in the US: **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline)

## What to Say — Examples of Helpful Language

| Situation | Helpful | Unhelpful |
|---|---|---|
| Someone is crying | *"It's okay. I'm here. Take your time."* | *"Don't cry, it's going to be fine."* |
| Someone is in shock/numb | *"You're safe now. Can I get you some water?"* | *"Snap out of it, you need to move."* |
| Someone wants to talk about what happened | Listen without interrupting. Reflect back. | *"Are you sure that's what happened?"* |
| Someone asks if their loved one is okay | *"I don't know yet, but here's how we can find out."* | *"Don't worry, they're probably fine."* |
| Someone feels guilty about surviving | *"A lot of people feel that way. You don't have to figure it out now."* | *"You shouldn't feel that way."* |

---

## Immediate Aftermath: What to Expect

People may experience:

- Numbness, shock, denial (very common in the first hours)
- Crying, anger, fear
- Confusion, disorientation
- Physical symptoms (shaking, nausea, chest tightness)
- Wanting to find family/friends/children urgently
- Not wanting to be alone

None of these are "wrong." They are normal responses to an abnormal event.

## Summary: The Short Version

1. **Make sure the scene is safe first.** Your safety matters.
2. **Approach calmly. Ask simple, respectful questions.**
3. **Listen. Don't force them to talk. Don't force them to tell their story.**
4. **Meet practical needs within your capacity.** Water, a seat, a call to a loved one.
5. **Give accurate information. Don't speculate.**
6. **Connect them with supports — family, friends, services.**
7. **Don't make false promises. Don't pathologize. Don't take photos.**
8. **Take care of yourself too. Know your limits. Escalate to professionals when needed.**
